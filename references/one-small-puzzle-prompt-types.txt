# One Small Puzzle — Puzzle types

---

Impossible state of an everyday object. Each puzzle asks the reader to identify or explain a depicted state that an ordinary, correctly functioning everyday object could never reach under the conditions shown. Build each instance around a different familiar relationship between parts, positions, readings or stages of an action. The image must supply the evidence a reader needs. If the answer depends on a particular setup, viewing direction or moment in time, make that condition clear. In the private solution, explain exactly what contradicts what and check plausible alternative explanations. When offering several choices, verify that each alternative is physically or functionally possible. Use varied objects and situations, and choose fresh ideas beyond the previously tested clock-face lineup.

---

Illustrated observation and error spotting. Create ten original illustrated scenes in which the reader must find and explain one specific visual error, inconsistency or out-of-place detail. Establish what belongs through evidence visible in the scene, such as an object’s function, a sequence of events or the relationship between two details. For harder puzzles, make several details look plausible at first glance and let a second clue distinguish the answer. Vary the settings, subjects and reasoning across the set. Keep the decisive clues visible at phone size and preserve the challenge through careful composition. Inspect the finished illustration for accidental errors that could create a second valid answer. In the private solution, identify the exact location, explain the contradiction and show why the strongest alternative fits the scene.

---

Counting shapes in overlapping figures or groups of objects. Create ten original puzzles that ask for one exact count. Vary what the reader must notice: a large figure assembled from smaller ones, a valid shape in an unexpected orientation, or an object that belongs to exactly two overlapping groups. Make the counting rule clear from the question and image, including which sizes or orientations qualify and what counts as a complete boundary. Build harder puzzles around combinations that are easy to overlook even when every detail is visible at phone size. Count each qualifying item once. In the private solution, account for every item by size, position or group membership, explain a plausible miscount, and verify the total against the finished image.

---

Reflection, rotation and cube nets. Create ten original puzzles that vary what the reader must mentally do: rotate an asymmetric shape to match another, identify a mirror image, or fold a marked net and decide which cube view is possible. State which transformations are allowed. For nets, specify which side faces outward and show enough of each marking to determine its orientation. Build harder choices that agree on an obvious feature, such as which faces meet, but differ in where a symbol points or which corner it touches. In the private solution, trace the decisive feature, explain why the closest alternative fails, and check every option against all permitted rotations or reflections. Verify those relationships on the finished image at phone size.

---

Matchsticks, pencils and other move puzzles. Create ten original puzzles in which the reader relocates a stated number of whole pieces to reach a precise goal. Vary the reasoning across the set by repairing an equation, changing the number of enclosed shapes, or finding the greatest or least value reachable. State whether the move count is exact or a maximum, whether pieces may rotate, which positions are legal and which symbols stay fixed. Make harder puzzles depend on what removing a piece changes at its source as well as what placing it achieves at its destination. In the private solution, identify each move and verify the final arrangement against the rendered starting image. Check uniqueness when promised. For an optimum, show a legal way to reach it and explain why no better result is possible.

---

Area dissection and geometric proof. Create ten original puzzles in which the reader must determine an area or prove a relationship between regions. Vary the decisive insight across the set: triangles with a shared altitude, pieces that can be rearranged, an area counted twice, or a strategically drawn line that reveals similar figures. Give every necessary condition in the text or through clear geometric markings. A reader should be able to solve each puzzle without measuring the drawing. For harder entries, make the answer depend on combining two facts whose connection is easy to overlook. In the private solution, justify each claimed equality or ratio and show how it yields the answer. Inspect the finished image at phone size to confirm that the labels, intersections, markings and shaded regions match the proof.

---

Number relationships, carries and overlapping sums. Create ten original puzzles in which the reader deduces a missing value or a forced total from the numbers shown. Vary the decisive insight across the set. A shared tile might be counted twice when two group totals are added, subtracting one total from another might cancel several unknowns, or a carry from the units column might force the tens digit. Make group boundaries and place values easy to read, and state which digits may be used and whether they may repeat. If several arrangements satisfy the clues, ask for a quantity they all share. In the private solution, show one valid arrangement, explain why the requested value is forced, and verify it against the clues in the finished image.

---

Number machines, sequences and symbol equations. Create ten original puzzles in which the reader determines a machine’s output, the next term in a sequence or the value of a symbol expression. Vary the reasoning across the set. One puzzle might require working backward through a machine, another might hinge on two alternating operations, and a third might reveal a symbol’s value when two equations are subtracted. For machines and sequences, define the allowed rules precisely enough that every rule fitting the clues gives the same requested answer. For symbol equations, state the allowed values and whether different symbols may share a value. Build harder puzzles around clues that must be combined. In the private solution, derive the answer, rule out competing results and check every input, term and symbol against the finished image at phone size.

---

Ordering, elimination and conflicting statements. Create ten original puzzles in which the reader deduces who came first, which assignment remains possible or whose statement must be false. Vary the decisive insight across the set. Two ordering clues might leave only one slot for a person, or a pair of contradictory statements might account for the only false claim. Make the rules explicit wherever they affect the answer. Distinguish “before” from “immediately before,” say whether ties or repeated assignments are allowed, and specify whether a truth condition applies to individual statements or to speakers. For harder puzzles, let two possibilities survive the first deduction and give the reader a further clue that separates them. In the private solution, show how the clues force the answer and check every arrangement consistent with the wording on the finished image. If several arrangements work, ask for a fact they all share.

---

Switches, neighbors and legal routes. Create ten original puzzles in which the reader chooses which switches to press, deduces a cell’s state from neighboring clues or constructs a route under stated rules. Show which cells a switch toggles, whether diagonal neighbors count and whether a route may revisit a cell or reuse an edge. Make harder puzzles depend on how constraints interact. Two switches might toggle the same light twice, or a legal turn might cut off the only way to reach a required checkpoint. Ask for a compact answer, such as a set of switch labels, a forced cell or the fewest moves needed. In the private solution, replay the presses, check the neighbor counts or trace the route on the finished image. Verify uniqueness when claimed, prove any stated minimum and check that every decisive marking is clear at phone size.

---

Fitting pieces together, reconciling views and proving a minimum. Create ten original puzzles in which the reader fits pieces into a target, works out which construction matches every labeled view or finds the fewest pieces that can satisfy all the clues. For fitting puzzles, state which pieces must be used, whether they may be rotated or flipped, and whether gaps or overlaps are allowed. For views, mark the viewing directions and state any support rule for hidden blocks. In harder puzzles, make two clues interact. A piece that fills one corner might leave an impossible gap elsewhere, or a single stack might satisfy height requirements in two views at once. In the private solution, show a valid construction and check it against the finished image at phone size. For a minimum, explain why every construction needs at least that many pieces and show one that attains the bound. Several constructions may attain the same minimum.

---

Language, age and everyday logic. Create ten original puzzles in which the reader resolves a sentence with two plausible meanings, works out someone’s age from clues about different points in time, or deduces what must have happened in a familiar situation. Vary the decisive insight across the set. A later remark might clarify whom “she” refers to, an age gap stays fixed even as an age ratio changes, or the order in which two coats were hung might explain which one can be taken off a hook first. Supply any fact the answer depends on, including birthday timing or how an object works. For harder puzzles, let two explanations fit the opening clue and make a second clue decide between them. In the private solution, show why the answer follows and test the strongest alternative against the exact wording and details visible in the finished image.
